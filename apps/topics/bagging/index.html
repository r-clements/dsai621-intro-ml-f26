Bagging and Random Forests

Part 1 averages bootstrap trees (bagging) and shows the variance falling. Part 2 adds the two random forest settings, the number of predictors tried at each split and the minimum leaf size, and shows the variance falling further.

Part 1. Bagging: averaging trees smooths the staircase

The data are Example B from the notes: n = 100 points with x uniform on [0, 1] and y = f(x) + noise, where f(x) = sin(12(x + 0.2)) / (x + 0.2) and the noise has variance 1. A fully grown regression tree fits a staircase that chases the noise. Bagging grows a fully grown tree on each of B bootstrap samples (n points drawn with replacement) and averages their predictions. Move the slider to average more trees. The top plot shows one training set: watch the staircase's jumps get smaller and the fit get smoother. The bottom plots repeat the whole thing on 200 training sets and show 30 of them: watch the band of fits narrow (the variance roughly halves) while their average stays almost exactly on f (bias rises only slightly). With a single predictor there is nothing for a random forest to hide at a split, so here a random forest and bagging are the same thing; Part 2 uses five predictors. (ISL 8.2.1; ESL 8.7.)

Keyboard and screen reader users: Tab to the slider and use the arrow keys to step through B = 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 and 200 trees (Home and End jump to 1 and 200). "Next training set" shows another of the 200 training sets in the top plot. The squared bias and variance are announced in the statistics line below the slider, they are also in the table at the end of Part 1, and each plot's description gives its numbers.

From 200 simulated training sets. Expected test MSE = 1 (the noise variance) + squared bias + variance. The squared bias is the squared gap between the average fit and f, minus the part of that gap due to averaging only 200 fits (the variance divided by 200); without that correction, a fully grown tree's tiny bias would be swamped by simulation noise.

Part 2. Random forests: decorrelating the trees

Part 1 stops at about half the variance because bagged trees are alike: they are grown on overlapping samples and all split first on the strongest predictor. A random forest lets each split choose from only m of the p predictors, drawn at random, so the trees differ more and their average varies less. A larger minimum leaf size stops each tree before it chases single points. The data are Example C from the notes: y = x1 + x2 + x3 + noise (variance 1), where x1, x2, x3 are standard normal with correlation 0.8 and x4, x5 are pure noise, so p = 5, with n = 200 and B = 100 trees. Five predictors can't be drawn, so the plots show every fit along the diagonal x1 = x2 = x3 = t (with x4 = x5 = 0), where most of the data lie and the truth is the line 3t. Start at m = 5 (bagging) and leaf size 1, then lower m and raise the leaf size: watch the band of forests on the right narrow below the bagged band on the left, until at m = 1 or large leaves the forests start missing the line (bias). (ISL 8.2.2; ESL 15.2–15.4.)

Keyboard and screen reader users: Tab to either slider and use the arrow keys. The first sets m from 1 to 5; the second sets the minimum leaf size to 1, 3, 5 or 10. "Next training set" shows another training set in the top plot of Part 2. The squared bias and variance of bagging and of the current forest are announced in the statistics line below the buttons, they are also in the table at the end of Part 2, and each plot's description gives its numbers.
